# Business Automation Specialist

## Course Overview

This career-oriented program prepares a developing learner to understand, perform, verify, document, and discuss entry-level work in business automation. It combines tool-independent principles with guided practice, realistic constraints, professional communication, troubleshooting, and portfolio evidence.

**Target roles:** Automation Assistant; No-Code Automation Specialist; Operations Support Specialist; Workflow Coordinator; Junior Solutions Implementer  
**Continuing workplace case:** Harbor & Pine Services, a 22-person service company that loses inquiries during manual handoffs  
**Typical tools and artifacts:** process maps, spreadsheets, form tools, workflow automation platforms, webhooks, APIs, logs, documentation tools  
**Recommended prerequisite:** confident computer use, professional written communication, spreadsheet fundamentals, and completion of the UPSTRIDE Digital Niche Assessment or equivalent reflection.  
**Estimated effort:** 120-160 hours including capstone and revision.

## Course Objectives

By successful completion, the learner can:

1. Explain the field, terminology, professional responsibilities, and business relevance.
2. Discover a problem, define requirements, and select an appropriate workflow.
3. Produce and test realistic entry-level work using relevant tools.
4. Diagnose common failures systematically and document corrections.
5. Communicate scope, progress, decisions, risk, and handoff professionally.
6. Present a portfolio-worthy capstone and respond to technical, scenario, and behavioral interview questions.

## Completion Requirements

- Complete all lesson activities and knowledge checks.
- Earn at least 75% on every module performance assessment.
- Achieve Competent in every critical capstone category.
- Submit the portfolio case study, presentation, interview practice, and job-readiness self-evaluation.
- Revise work when critical feedback identifies a correctness, safety, privacy, testing, or documentation gap.

## Module 1 - Automation Work and Professional Responsibility

**Purpose:** Build the knowledge, workflow, judgment, and evidence required to use automation purpose, limits, ethics, privacy, role boundaries, and outcome definition in an entry-level professional context.

**Module outcome:** By the end of this module, the learner can explain and apply automation purpose, limits, ethics, privacy, role boundaries, and outcome definition, complete a realistic work product, assess its quality and risk, and communicate the result professionally.

**Skills developed:** analysis; tool-independent reasoning; professional execution; testing; documentation; communication; reflection.

**Job relevance:** This module prepares learners to complete, explain, and defend work involving automation work and professional responsibility under realistic supervision and quality expectations.

### Lesson 1.1 - Automation Work and Professional Responsibility: Concepts and Professional Context

**Estimated effort:** 90-150 minutes  
**Why this lesson matters:** Professionals cannot deliver automation work and professional responsibility reliably when they know tool steps but cannot explain the purpose, limits, or consequences of their decisions.

#### Learning objectives
- Explain the essential ideas within automation purpose, limits, ethics, privacy, role boundaries, and outcome definition.
- Apply a professional method to a realistic business automation task.
- Identify quality, risk, communication, and documentation requirements before completing the work.

#### Core instruction

The working subject of this lesson is **automation purpose, limits, ethics, privacy, role boundaries, and outcome definition**. Begin by defining the business or customer outcome before selecting a tool or action. Gather observable evidence, separate facts from assumptions, and identify the people affected by the work. A technically possible action is not automatically appropriate: consider permissions, privacy, accessibility, security, cost, maintainability, and the ability of the team to operate the result.

Use a five-part reasoning pattern: **Outcome -> Evidence -> Options -> Decision -> Verification**. State the outcome in measurable or observable terms. Collect enough evidence to understand the current condition. Generate realistic options, including the option to simplify or defer. Select an option using explicit criteria. Finally, define how a reviewer will know the work is correct and what should happen if it fails.

Professionals make their reasoning visible. Maintain a short decision record containing the request, context, constraints, assumptions, selected approach, rejected alternatives, risks, owner, due date, and acceptance evidence. This protects continuity and makes feedback more useful. Tool screenshots alone are not sufficient documentation because they rarely explain why the configuration exists.

#### Professional workflow
1. Clarify the request and the outcome that matters.
2. Inspect the current state and collect representative evidence.
3. Identify constraints, risks, dependencies, and people affected.
4. Compare viable approaches using stated criteria.
5. Complete the work in a controlled or test context.
6. Verify the result against acceptance criteria and edge cases.
7. Document the decision, result, limitations, owner, and next review.

#### Worked example

In the continuing case, **Harbor & Pine Services, a 22-person service company that loses inquiries during manual handoffs**, a stakeholder asks for a quick change related to automation work and professional responsibility. The learner first rewrites the request as an outcome, identifies missing information, and inspects a representative sample. Two solutions are compared: a rapid narrow fix and a more durable redesign. The recommendation explains the trade-off, contains a test and rollback plan, and gives the stakeholder a concise update rather than silently implementing the first idea.

#### Common mistakes
- Starting with a preferred tool before defining the problem.
- Treating one successful test as proof that the system works in normal and exceptional conditions.
- Ignoring ownership, permissions, privacy, accessibility, or downstream users.
- Documenting clicks without recording decisions, assumptions, and acceptance evidence.

#### Best-practice standard

Work should be **purposeful, evidence-based, reversible where practical, tested, documented, and owned**. When uncertainty is material, escalate it instead of hiding it.

#### Practical activity

Create a **automation-work-and-professional-responsibility decision packet** for the course case. Include a one-paragraph problem statement, current-state evidence, two options, decision criteria, recommendation, risk list, acceptance checks, and a professional status message. The output must be understandable without an oral explanation.

#### Knowledge check
1. Why should the outcome be defined before selecting a method for automation work and professional responsibility?
2. What evidence would distinguish a symptom from the underlying problem?
3. Which quality and risk checks are essential before release?
4. How would you explain a trade-off to a non-specialist stakeholder?

**Answer standard:** A strong response explains cause and effect, names relevant evidence, recognizes trade-offs, and describes verification. A definition without application is incomplete.

#### Application challenge

The client changes one important requirement two days before the agreed handoff. Decide whether to accept, defer, or redesign the work. Produce a change note that describes impact on scope, time, quality, risk, and acceptance. Do not assume that “the client asked” removes professional responsibility.

#### Workplace and hiring connection

This skill may appear in a work-sample test, scenario interview, client update, quality review, or troubleshooting discussion. Be ready to explain what you did, why you selected the approach, how you tested it, and what you would improve.

#### Key takeaways

- Define the outcome before the method.
- Use evidence and explicit criteria to make decisions.
- Test normal, boundary, and failure conditions.
- Communicate and document work so another professional can continue it.

It works locally, saves progress in the learner's browser, calculates a provisional score, and exports a JSON submission for the learning system. **Why this lab is connected:** The module teaches **Automation Work and Professional Responsibility**. The lab turns that knowledge into four linked tables. The Project Brief defines the intended outcome; the Build Log records the learner's implementation choices; Test Evidence checks whether the work meets the brief; and the Submission Summary explains what the evidence means. If the brief changes, the build and tests must be updated. If a test fails, the summary must disclose it and propose correction.

| Table | Field | What the learner enters | Why it matters | Connection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Project Brief | Objective | One observable business or user outcome | Prevents activity from being confused with value | Becomes the basis of acceptance tests |
| Project Brief | Current state | Evidence of how work happens now | Grounds the solution in reality | Explains why each build step exists |
| Project Brief | Inputs and constraints | Data, people, tools, limits, permissions | Prevents unsafe or impossible designs | Controls build choices and test boundaries |
| Build Log | Step and decision | What was created and why | Makes thinking reviewable | Must trace back to the brief |
| Build Log | Owner and status | Responsible person and completion state | Creates accountability | Feeds the submission completeness check |
| Build Log | Risk or exception | Failure condition and planned response | Builds professional judgment | Must have related test evidence |
| Test Evidence | Test case | Normal, boundary, failure, and permission scenario | Demonstrates more than a happy path | Verifies the objective and constraints |
| Test Evidence | Expected vs. actual | Predicted and observed result | Reveals defects objectively | Determines pass, revise, or escalate |
| Test Evidence | Evidence reference | Screenshot name, test record, or safe demo link | Makes claims inspectable | Supports automated and instructor review |
| Submission Summary | Result | What works, what does not, and limitations | Prevents overclaiming | Synthesizes all preceding tables |
| Submission Summary | Reflection | Decision, feedback, and next improvement | Shows learning and judgment | Contributes to the course points |

5. Add at least three tests: one normal case, one boundary or missing-data case, and one failure or permission case.

### Lesson 3.1 - Opportunity Analysis and Solution Prioritization: Concepts and Professional Context

**Estimated effort:** 90-150 minutes  
**Why this lesson matters:** Professionals cannot deliver opportunity analysis and solution prioritization reliably when they know tool steps but cannot explain the purpose, limits, or consequences of their decisions.

#### Learning objectives
- Explain the essential ideas within volume, effort, stability, value, feasibility, risk, and automation readiness.
- Apply a professional method to a realistic business automation task.
- Identify quality, risk, communication, and documentation requirements before completing the work.

#### Core instruction

The working subject of this lesson is **volume, effort, stability, value, feasibility, risk, and automation readiness**. Begin by defining the business or customer outcome before selecting a tool or action. Gather observable evidence, separate facts from assumptions, and identify the people affected by the work. A technically possible action is not automatically appropriate: consider permissions, privacy, accessibility, security, cost, maintainability, and the ability of the team to operate the result.

Use a five-part reasoning pattern: **Outcome -> Evidence -> Options -> Decision -> Verification**. State the outcome in measurable or observable terms. Collect enough evidence to understand the current condition. Generate realistic options, including the option to simplify or defer. Select an option using explicit criteria. Finally, define how a reviewer will know the work is correct and what should happen if it fails.

Professionals make their reasoning visible. Maintain a short decision record containing the request, context, constraints, assumptions, selected approach, rejected alternatives, risks, owner, due date, and acceptance evidence. This protects continuity and makes feedback more useful. Tool screenshots alone are not sufficient documentation because they rarely explain why the configuration exists.

#### Professional workflow
1. Clarify the request and the outcome that matters.
2. Inspect the current state and collect representative evidence.
3. Identify constraints, risks, dependencies, and people affected.
4. Compare viable approaches using stated criteria.
5. Complete the work in a controlled or test context.
6. Verify the result against acceptance criteria and edge cases.
7. Document the decision, result, limitations, owner, and next review.

#### Worked example

### Lesson 8.1 - Documentation, Adoption, and Client Handoff: Concepts and Professional Context

**Estimated effort:** 90-150 minutes  
**Why this lesson matters:** Professionals cannot deliver documentation, adoption, and client handoff reliably when they know tool steps but cannot explain the purpose, limits, or consequences of their decisions.

#### Learning objectives
- Explain the essential ideas within SOPs, system diagrams, ownership, training, change management, and support boundaries.
- Apply a professional method to a realistic business automation task.
- Identify quality, risk, communication, and documentation requirements before completing the work.

#### Core instruction

The working subject of this lesson is **SOPs, system diagrams, ownership, training, change management, and support boundaries**. Begin by defining the business or customer outcome before selecting a tool or action. Gather observable evidence, separate facts from assumptions, and identify the people affected by the work. A technically possible action is not automatically appropriate: consider permissions, privacy, accessibility, security, cost, maintainability, and the ability of the team to operate the result.

Use a five-part reasoning pattern: **Outcome -> Evidence -> Options -> Decision -> Verification**. State the outcome in measurable or observable terms. Collect enough evidence to understand the current condition. Generate realistic options, including the option to simplify or defer. Select an option using explicit criteria. Finally, define how a reviewer will know the work is correct and what should happen if it fails.

Professionals make their reasoning visible. Maintain a short decision record containing the request, context, constraints, assumptions, selected approach, rejected alternatives, risks, owner, due date, and acceptance evidence. This protects continuity and makes feedback more useful. Tool screenshots alone are not sufficient documentation because they rarely explain why the configuration exists.

#### Professional workflow
1. Clarify the request and the outcome that matters.
2. Inspect the current state and collect representative evidence.
3. Identify constraints, risks, dependencies, and people affected.
4. Compare viable approaches using stated criteria.
5. Complete the work in a controlled or test context.
6. Verify the result against acceptance criteria and edge cases.
7. Document the decision, result, limitations, owner, and next review.

#### Worked example

In the continuing case, **Harbor & Pine Services, a 22-person service company that loses inquiries during manual handoffs**, a stakeholder asks for a quick change related to documentation, adoption, and client handoff. The learner first rewrites the request as an outcome, identifies missing information, and inspects a representative sample. Two solutions are compared: a rapid narrow fix and a more durable redesign. The recommendation explains the trade-off, contains a test and rollback plan, and gives the stakeholder a concise update rather than silently implementing the first idea.

#### Common mistakes
- Starting with a preferred tool before defining the problem.
- Treating one successful test as proof that the system works in normal and exceptional conditions.
- Ignoring ownership, permissions, privacy, accessibility, or downstream users.
- Documenting clicks without recording decisions, assumptions, and acceptance evidence.

#### Best-practice standard

Work should be **purposeful, evidence-based, reversible where practical, tested, documented, and owned**. When uncertainty is material, escalate it instead of hiding it.

#### Practical activity

Create a **documentation-adoption-and-client-handoff decision packet** for the course case. Include a one-paragraph problem statement, current-state evidence, two options, decision criteria, recommendation, risk list, acceptance checks, and a professional status message. The output must be understandable without an oral explanation.

#### Knowledge check
1. Why should the outcome be defined before selecting a method for documentation, adoption, and client handoff?
2. What evidence would distinguish a symptom from the underlying problem?
3. Which quality and risk checks are essential before release?
4. How would you explain a trade-off to a non-specialist stakeholder?

**Answer standard:** A strong response explains cause and effect, names relevant evidence, recognizes trade-offs, and describes verification. A definition without application is incomplete.

#### Application challenge

The client changes one important requirement two days before the agreed handoff. Decide whether to accept, defer, or redesign the work. Produce a change note that describes impact on scope, time, quality, risk, and acceptance. Do not assume that “the client asked” removes professional responsibility.

#### Workplace and hiring connection

This skill may appear in a work-sample test, scenario interview, client update, quality review, or troubleshooting discussion. Be ready to explain what you did, why you selected the approach, how you tested it, and what you would improve.

#### Key takeaways

- Define the outcome before the method.
- Use evidence and explicit criteria to make decisions.
- Test normal, boundary, and failure conditions.
- Communicate and document work so another professional can continue it.

### Lesson 10.1 - Capstone, Portfolio, and Automation Career Readiness: Concepts and Professional Context

**Estimated effort:** 90-150 minutes  
**Why this lesson matters:** Professionals cannot deliver capstone, portfolio, and automation career readiness reliably when they know tool steps but cannot explain the purpose, limits, or consequences of their decisions.

#### Learning objectives
- Explain the essential ideas within integrated delivery, evidence, case-study writing, demonstration, interviews, and improvement planning.
- Apply a professional method to a realistic business automation task.
- Identify quality, risk, communication, and documentation requirements before completing the work.

#### Core instruction

The working subject of this lesson is **integrated delivery, evidence, case-study writing, demonstration, interviews, and improvement planning**. Begin by defining the business or customer outcome before selecting a tool or action. Gather observable evidence, separate facts from assumptions, and identify the people affected by the work. A technically possible action is not automatically appropriate: consider permissions, privacy, accessibility, security, cost, maintainability, and the ability of the team to operate the result.

Use a five-part reasoning pattern: **Outcome -> Evidence -> Options -> Decision -> Verification**. State the outcome in measurable or observable terms. Collect enough evidence to understand the current condition. Generate realistic options, including the option to simplify or defer. Select an option using explicit criteria. Finally, define how a reviewer will know the work is correct and what should happen if it fails.

Professionals make their reasoning visible. Maintain a short decision record containing the request, context, constraints, assumptions, selected approach, rejected alternatives, risks, owner, due date, and acceptance evidence. This protects continuity and makes feedback more useful. Tool screenshots alone are not sufficient documentation because they rarely explain why the configuration exists.

#### Professional workflow
1. Clarify the request and the outcome that matters.
2. Inspect the current state and collect representative evidence.
3. Identify constraints, risks, dependencies, and people affected.
4. Compare viable approaches using stated criteria.
5. Complete the work in a controlled or test context.
6. Verify the result against acceptance criteria and edge cases.
7. Document the decision, result, limitations, owner, and next review.

#### Worked example

In the continuing case, **Harbor & Pine Services, a 22-person service company that loses inquiries during manual handoffs**, a stakeholder asks for a quick change related to capstone, portfolio, and automation career readiness. The recommendation explains the trade-off, contains a test and rollback plan, and gives the stakeholder a concise update rather than silently implementing the first idea.

#### Common mistakes

- Treating one successful test as proof that the system works in normal and exceptional conditions.
- Ignoring ownership, permissions, privacy, accessibility, or downstream users.
- Documenting clicks without recording decisions, assumptions, and acceptance evidence.

#### Best-practice standard

## Tool Module T3 — Zapier

**Outcome:** Create an accessible small-business automation and evaluate when Zapier is the better fit.

**Official learning source:** https://help.zapier.com/

### Lessons

1. **Fit and boundaries:** capabilities, suitable use cases, plan/access considerations, data responsibility, and when another tool is more appropriate.
2. **Guided build:** reproduce the supplied safe demo using fictional data and record every configuration decision.
3. **Testing and troubleshooting:** test normal, boundary, failure, duplicate, and permission conditions; inspect history or logs where available.
4. **Professional handoff:** document ownership, operating steps, limitations, monitoring, and recovery.
